---
title: "On Noticing"
tags: [attention, perception]
---

The first thing I registered in Vancouver was the air. Not the mountains, not the buildings — the smell of the place, trees and sea and something cold underneath it. I had watched videos of that city for years. I knew what it looked like. Nobody had mentioned what it smelled like, and that turned out to be the thing I couldn't stop noticing. Weeks in, it still arrived new every morning. I never got used to it.

That bothered me a little, in a way I couldn't explain at the time. Of everything I saw there, why did the air get through?

I've been circling the question since, and it's turned into a broader one. What makes something worth noticing?

Seeing is not noticing

Start with a distinction that sounds obvious and isn't.

Seeing happens to you. Light hits your eyes all day and most of it never reaches you in any meaningful sense. You walk the same street a hundred times and couldn't describe a single storefront. You sit across from someone and don't register that they've changed their hair. The senses were working the whole time. Nothing was noticed.

Noticing is something you do. It means stepping out of autopilot and giving something your attention on purpose. It's deliberate in a way seeing never is.

Most of life is seen. Very little of it is noticed. And I think the gap between those two verbs is where a lot of what we call presence actually lives.

Two filters

So what gets through? I've come to think there are two filters, and something has to pass both.

The first is surprise. Your mind is constantly predicting what comes next. Most of the time it's right, and what's correctly predicted goes unregistered — that's the whole point of prediction, to save you the trouble of processing the expected. What breaks the prediction is what gets through. Sometimes it breaks by exceeding what you expected, the way a piece of music can be more beautiful than the moment called for. Sometimes it breaks by violating it, the way a friend's voice going slightly flat tells you something before they say a word.

This is why routine is so hostile to noticing. Routine is prediction that keeps being right. Nothing breaks, so nothing registers, and the days go smooth and blank.

But surprise alone isn't enough. A car alarm is surprising. Nobody stands still on the sidewalk contemplating a car alarm.

The second filter is care. What we stay with is what touches a feeling we already value — often without knowing we value it. Awe. Wonder. Tenderness. Concern for someone. Sometimes just the wish to be safe. The thing doesn't have to be beautiful, and it doesn't have to be something we were looking for. It has to land somewhere that already matters to us.

Surprise makes something catchable. Care makes it keepable.

That's what the Vancouver air was doing. It broke a prediction I didn't know I'd made — I had built an entire picture of that city out of images, and images carry no smell. And it landed on something I cared about more than I'd admitted to myself, which was the question of whether I could belong somewhere that wasn't where I was born.

Being caught, and choosing to stay

There's a second thing hidden inside noticing that I kept missing: it happens in two moments, not one.

The first is being caught. That isn't a choice. Something arrests you before any decision is made — the light doing something unusual on a wall, a phrase in a conversation that snags. You don't decide to be caught. You just are.

The second is staying. That one is entirely a choice. You can let the moment slide past, which is what usually happens, or you can stop and stay with it for a few seconds longer than necessary.

Almost everyone gets caught constantly. Almost nobody stays. And I've come to think the staying is where the whole value of noticing lives, because the staying is the only part you control.

What the staying does to you

Here's what I was wrong about at the start. I assumed the value was in the thing — that some objects and moments are inherently worth attention, and noticing is just the act of correctly identifying them.

I don't think that anymore. The value is in what the act does to the person doing it.

Iris Murdoch described a moment of standing at a window, anxious and turned in on herself, when a hawk appeared outside. For a few seconds there was no self-pity, no brooding, nothing but the bird. She called it unselfing, and she thought it was close to the center of moral life: the ego briefly displaced by something real that isn't you.

That's what a genuine act of noticing does. It isn't control over the moment — that's how I first wanted to describe it, and it's wrong. Deep noticing feels much more like being overtaken. It's freedom from the automatic, not authority over it. For a few seconds you're not running your own commentary, and the relief of that is part of why it feels so good.

It also compounds. Every real act of attention makes the next one slightly more likely. Notice the light in your kitchen this week and you're marginally more likely to notice it next week. Attention is trainable, which means the kind of person you are is partly downstream of what you've practiced attending to.

And pointed at a person, it stops being private. Simone Weil called attention the rarest and purest form of generosity, and I think that's exactly right. Most people move through whole days without anyone truly registering them. Being noticed — not evaluated, just received — is one of the few things we can give each other for free that can't be faked.

What makes it hard

Three things work against all of this, and they're worth naming because they're mostly invisible.

Attention is finite. "Worth noticing" is a literal phrase. You cannot attend to everything, so every act of noticing spends something, and what you spend it on is a statement about your priorities whether or not you meant it as one.

Habit erodes it. Anything constant eventually becomes invisible. This is the mechanism that makes people stop seeing their own homes, their own cities, the people they live with. A Russian critic once argued that this is what art is for — to make the familiar strange again, to make the stone feel like stone. Every photographer and poet is essentially forcing an audience to notice something they'd stopped seeing.

Capturing can replace it. This one I'm still guilty of. Taking a photograph can quietly stand in for looking. The camera takes over the job of remembering, and the mind, relieved of the task, doesn't bother. The photo exists. The noticing didn't happen.

What it says about you

Here's where it lands for me.

If something has to touch what you care about before you'll stay with it, then what you habitually notice is a readout of what you value. Not what you say you value — what you actually do.

Three people walk down the same street. One notices the buildings. One notices the man sitting against the wall asking for change. One notices nothing at all, because they're rehearsing an argument in their head. They saw the same street. What differed was what each of them was tuned to care about.

Which means attention isn't only a way of experiencing the world. It's evidence. And the slightly uncomfortable corollary is that if you want to know what someone values, you'd learn more from watching where their attention goes than from asking them.

Including yourself. Especially yourself.

This started as a journal entry and became an argument through a long conversation. The two-filter idea came out of that back-and-forth; I'd only had half of it on my own.
